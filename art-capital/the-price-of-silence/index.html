The Price of Silence

Monetary policy surprises, auction-market liquidity and the hidden distribution of art investment risk

Research edition · 7 November 2026 · Public-data pilot · Not investment advice

Research question

Can unexpected monetary tightening increase the dispersion of art-investment outcomes—not merely lower their average—by reducing sell-through, changing which works transact and potentially separating trophy assets from the middle market? We test what public evidence permits and explicitly identify what cannot yet be estimated.

Evidence boundary. Historical HAUS aggregates below are observed public data. The monetary-surprise regression uses embedded official event aggregates. Annual median hammer prices are not investment returns; quantile responses, causal effects and the masterpiece premium remain unestimated.

03 · Economic theory: why an art price is not an exit price

Art is a heterogeneous, infrequently traded asset. A recorded auction price is conditional on a transaction taking place; it is not necessarily the price at which an owner could liquidate an otherwise comparable work today. Four complementary theories explain why monetary shocks may alter observed art-market outcomes.

01 / SELECTION

Transaction-selection bias

When sellers withdraw works or lots fail to meet reserve prices, the recorded sales sample changes. The median price of sold artworks can remain stable even as the probability of sale falls. This is a selection mechanism to test, not proof that the underlying market value held firm.

Test: sold-versus-unsold lot characteristics, estimates, reserves and repeat sales.

02 / LIQUIDITY

Liquidity preference and market depth

When financing becomes more expensive or uncertain, buyers may demand a larger concession for assets that cannot be sold quickly. The relevant risk is both a lower executable price and a longer time to exit.

Test: bid participation, failed sales, time to resale and transaction discounts.

03 / DISCOUNTING

Opportunity cost and discount rates

Higher real yields can increase the opportunity cost of holding an asset without contractual cash flows. Yet art prices also depend on wealth, taste, scarcity and the composition of bidders; a single rate coefficient cannot capture all these channels.

Test: surprise rate shocks alongside real yields, wealth proxies and ECB conditions.

04 / MEASUREMENT

Smoothed valuations and apparent diversification

Infrequent transactions and appraisal-based marks can understate volatility and contemporaneous correlation with financial markets. Apparent resilience may partly reflect stale prices rather than genuinely independent risk.

Test: repeat-sales indices, appraisal-to-sale gaps and lag-adjusted correlations.

Competing explanations matter. Stable observed prices may reflect genuine scarcity, changing artwork quality, seller reserve discipline or selection of only the strongest works. The present HAUS annual data cannot distinguish these mechanisms causally.

07 · Methodology, reproducibility & limitations

Historical source: HAUS Kunstiindeks, archive vintage 10 October 2026. Annual records: archived lots, sold lots, priced sales, hammer totals, median hammer and hammer/start multiple. Sell-through = sold / archived lots. Source values are transcribed and exportable above. The monetary module uses embedded annual FOMC surprise aggregates derived from the Bauer–Swanson event-level series (2015–2023); no CSV import is required. It matches available annual observations to the auction series and estimates the descriptive slope as covariance divided by shock variance. The year 2020 is excluded from the matched regression because only one orthogonalized event observation is available for that year, making its annual aggregate non-comparable. Correlation and R² are calculated on the matched sample. Fewer than four matched years, or zero variation, suppresses estimation.

Key threats: small annual sample (11 years), serial dependence, omitted variables, survivorship/selection, changing auction composition, unverified archive completeness, country-specific policy exposure and a lack of repeat-sales identifiers. HC3 normal-approximation confidence intervals and leave-one-year-out sensitivity are computed from the embedded matched annual observations, but small-sample inference is fragile. Quantile regression and causal identification require richer data; no unverified statistical significance is displayed.

Primary source: San Francisco Fed Monetary Policy Surprises. Additional resource: US Monetary Policy Event-Study Database (updated 30 October 2026). Data rights and scope should be checked before reuse.
