## The Tax-to-GDP Signal: Fiscal Space or Composition Effect?

**02 November 2026 | Independent institutional research**

**The decision question.** How much does a hypothetical GDP shock change receipts at an unchanged tax-to-GDP ratio—and what if the ratio shifts?

**Executive assessment.** Eurostat’s 29 October release shows an increase in the EU tax-to-GDP ratio to 40.7% in 2025, while the Nordic comparison is not uniform: Denmark stood at 44.3%, whereas Sweden declined from 41.8% to 41.2%. The fiscal interpretation requires caution. A ratio can rise because of discretionary policy, changes in tax bases, composition effects or nominal GDP movements, without establishing that public finances have become more resilient. The dashboard separates the observed ratio from a transparent hypothetical nominal-GDP and ratio-shift scenario. For decision-makers, the key next step is to reconcile revenue buoyancy with expenditure rigidity, debt costs and demographic commitments rather than treating the ratio as a stand-alone fiscal-space metric.

**Worked example.** Illustrative worked example: at a hypothetical €500bn nominal GDP and the published EU 40.7% tax-to-GDP ratio, a 2% nominal GDP decline with an unchanged ratio mechanically reduces receipts by €4.07bn.

**Recommended action.** Separate cyclical revenue sensitivity from policy, compliance, base and composition effects. Evaluate expenditure commitments and debt-service dynamics before interpreting a tax-ratio increase as fiscal space.

**Evidence constraints.** The published tax-to-GDP ratio includes taxes and net social contributions; it is not a marginal tax rate. EU, EA20, Denmark and Sweden are not interchangeable jurisdictions. All GDP levels and shocks are illustrative and are not forecasts or budget projections.
